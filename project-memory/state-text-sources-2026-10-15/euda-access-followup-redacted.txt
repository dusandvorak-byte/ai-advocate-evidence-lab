Subject: Fundamental objection to EUDA response Ref. EXO/FHP/pd (26) D 106 of 25
September 2026 – comparability, objectivity and reliability of data on THC content in
cannabis
Dear Dr Nolan,
Thank you for EUDA’s extensive response of 25 September 2026 to my formal call to act
of 7 August 2026 and its subsequent supplements.
 Your response constitutes EUDA’s position on my call to act under Article 265 TFEU. I
therefore do not claim in this submission that procedural inaction within the meaning of
that provision continued after 25 September 2026. I do, however, raise a fundamental
objection to the reasons set out in your response and, in particular, to the evidently
unresolved problem of the governance, control, interpretation and publication of
European data on THC content in cannabis.
I. EUDA’s duties under Regulation (EU) 2023/1322
 The starting point cannot be whether EUDA is a superior authority over national forensic
laboratories. I have never claimed that it is.
The starting point must be EUDA’s own statutory duties.
 Regulation (EU) 2023/1322 of the European Parliament and of the Council requires EUDA
to provide the Union and the Member States with factual, objective, reliable and
comparable information on drugs and drug-related issues.
Article 6(1)(f) further requires the Agency to ensure improved comparability, objectivity
and reliability of information and data at Union level, inter alia through indicators and non-
binding common standards, with the aim of achieving greater uniformity of the
measurement methods used by Member States and the Union.
 Article 15 establishes a network of forensic and toxicological laboratories whose purpose
includes supporting quality-assurance systems and the further harmonisation of data-
collection and analytical methods.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 5 / 10>
My question is therefore not: “Why does EUDA not control the Czech police?”
 My question is: “How does EUDA fulfil its own statutory duty to ensure the objectivity,
reliability and comparability of data at Union level if it does not know and does not
systematically compare the analytical procedures by which the national values entering
European indicators were produced?”
II. EUDA’s response confirms the very core of the problem
 In its response, EUDA expressly acknowledges that methodological differences may be
relevant to the results of THC determination. This concerns, in particular, differences
between gas and liquid chromatography, the analytical treatment of THC and THCA,
sample preparation, homogenisation, drying and storage, representativeness of
sampling, and the determination and reporting of measurement uncertainty.


At the same time, EUDA expressly stated that it does not maintain a systematic inventory
of the analytical methods used by individual forensic laboratories in the Member States
for determining THC in cannabis.
 According to its own response, EUDA therefore does not hold comprehensive information
enabling it to compare the use of GC, GC-FID, LC or HPLC, approaches to THC and THCA
determination, sampling and storage procedures, or approaches to determining and
reporting measurement uncertainty.
The Agency also stated that it has not undertaken a systematic comparative assessment
of the THC determination methods used in the Member States.
 Furthermore, since its formal launch at the end of 2025, EUDA’s Network of Forensic and
Toxicological Laboratories has not undertaken dedicated work on methods for
determining THC concentrations in cannabis, THC/THCA determination, or the
harmonisation of those analytical approaches.
 Finally, EUDA stated that it has not carried out the comparative assessment of the issues
I raised, has not decided to conduct or commission such work, and that no such activity
is currently included in the Agency’s planning.
 This is precisely where the fundamental contradiction arises that your response does not
resolve.
III. This is not only about national forensic laboratories. It is about data published by
EUDA itself
 EUDA publishes European indicators, averages and time series concerning THC content
in seized cannabis.
If such figures are to be used as European indicators and are intended to enable
monitoring over time or comparison of data, it must be clear what the individual national
values actually mean and whether they measure a sufficiently comparable quantity.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 6 / 10>
Accreditation of a laboratory under ISO/IEC 17025 does not in itself resolve this issue.
EUDA itself acknowledged that the information on accreditation and proficiency testing
was not collected for the purpose of assessing the comparability of THC/THCA methods
and therefore does not permit a conclusion on that specific question.
 A laboratory may be technically competent to perform its own validated method without
this proving that its result is analytically comparable, without further qualification, to the
result of another laboratory using a different definition of the analyte, different sample
preparation or a different chromatographic procedure.
This is precisely the problem to which I am drawing attention.
IV. THC and THCA are not the same analytical question under different procedures
During gas chromatography, high temperature may cause decarboxylation of THCA to
THC. Liquid chromatography, by contrast, makes it possible to determine THC and THCA
separately.
The result therefore depends not only on the plant itself, but also on what was sampled,
how the sample was prepared, whether it was dried, how it was homogenised, how it was
stored, which analytical method was used, and whether the reported value is THC, THCA
or calculated “total THC”.


If individual Member States or their laboratories proceed differently, comparability of the
resulting figures cannot simply be assumed. It must either be methodologically
demonstrated or the limitations on comparability must be stated clearly and visibly when
the data are published.
V. This is not a theoretical issue
 Professor Lumír Ondřej Hanuš, an internationally recognised Czech chemist and
cannabinoid researcher who has long worked at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, drew
attention in his expert statement of 13 March 2015 to the fundamental importance of the
manner in which cannabinoids are determined and to the distinction between THC and
its acidic precursor THCA.
In the Czech Republic, the determination of active substances in cannabis was also
addressed by doc. RNDr. Peter Ondra, Ph.D., Head of the Institute of Forensic Medicine
and Medical Law at University Hospital Olomouc, who in communications of 29 April
2013 and 24 September 2014 confirmed measurements of active substances in cannabis
within our “Cannabis is Medicine” research programme. The submitted results also show
how markedly results may differ according to the analysed material and the procedure
selected.
 The expert institute of Palacký University Olomouc also concluded in its expert opinion
of 10 August 2015 that the submitted cannabis was not a precursor within the meaning of
the relevant legislation.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 7 / 10>
I did not submit these materials to EUDA so that it would decide guilt or innocence in my
criminal proceedings. I submitted them as concrete evidence that the definition of the
measured quantity and the analytical method may have fundamental practical and legal
consequences.
 The same is demonstrated by examples from other countries, including the materials
from Denmark.
VI. The Danish material shows why it is not enough to say that this is a national
competence
An official document of the Danish Ministry of Justice dated 28 June 2024 was also
submitted to EUDA.
Its significance is not that EUDA should review a specific Danish criminal case.
Its significance is that the document shows that the method of laboratory determination
of THC may have a direct bearing on legal classification and criminal-law consequences.
 If the same European terms such as “THC content”, “cannabis potency” or similar
indicators may be based on analytically different values, this is relevant not only to
national criminal law but also to the quality and interpretation of European data.
VII. Historical and EU-law context
The Czech Republic joined the European Union on 1 May 2004 and assumed the
obligation to apply the Union acquis.
Council Framework Decision 2004/757/JHA was adopted on 25 October 2004.
I do not claim that the Treaty of Accession or Article 83 TFEU themselves prescribed a
specific chromatographic method for measuring THC. Such a claim would be inaccurate.
 I do, however, draw attention to a broader problem: European harmonisation of drug
policy and the criminal-law framework proceeded without simultaneously ensuring
unequivocal European comparability of the analytical basis on which some cannabis-
related data may rest.


This is precisely why EUDA’s duty under Regulation (EU) 2023/1322 to improve the
comparability, objectivity and reliability of information and to support greater uniformity
of measurement methods is of particular importance today.
VIII. The fundamental question remains unanswered
I respect EUDA’s position that neither Article 6(1)(f) nor Article 15 of Regulation (EU)
2023/1322 requires the Agency, at the request of an individual, to carry out the particular
study proposed by that individual.
That, however, does not answer the essential question.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 8 / 10>
EUDA has scientific discretion in selecting specific scientific tools and priorities. In my
view, however, that discretion cannot mean that the Agency need not be able to
demonstrate how it fulfils the statutory duty itself to ensure improved comparability,
objectivity and reliability of the data that it processes and publishes at European level.
I therefore ask you to answer the following question:
 By what concrete and verifiable mechanism does EUDA ensure the comparability of
published data on THC content in cannabis between Member States and across time
series when EUDA simultaneously confirms that it has no systematic inventory of the THC
determination methods used by individual forensic laboratories, no comprehensive
information on differences between GC/GC-FID/LC/HPLC, THC/THCA, sampling, storage
and measurement uncertainty, and has not carried out a systematic comparative
assessment of those methods?
IX. Specific requests to EUDA
I therefore request that EUDA:
1. describe the concrete mechanism by which it verifies the comparability of national
data on THC content before using them in European averages, indicators and time series;
2. state what definition of THC is used for individual national inputs – in particular
whether it is native Δ9-THC, THC after thermal conversion of THCA, or “total THC” – and
how this is recorded in the metadata;
3. explain how, in European comparisons, it takes account of differences in sampling,
homogenisation, drying, storage, analytical method and measurement uncertainty;
4. explain how EUDA alerts users, when publishing European data, to limitations on their
comparability where the methodological comparability of national inputs has not been
verified;
5. state whether the two attached documents and the expert materials I submitted have
been forwarded to the expert team responsible for cannabis potency indicators and to the
Network of Forensic and Toxicological Laboratories under Article 15 of Regulation (EU)
2023/1322; if not, I request that they be forwarded and that the further course of action
be substantively reasoned;


6. provide a methodological audit trail at least for the selected 2024 figure and the 2014–
2024 time series: the original national datum, the definition of measured THC, the form of
material analysed, the aggregation method, the analytical method used, available
information on measurement uncertainty and recorded methodological changes; at the
same time, I request information on who verified the methodological comparability of the
Czech and Danish inputs and when; if it was not verified, I request that EUDA expressly
state this alongside the relevant indicator;
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 9 / 10>
7. state whether EUDA will review the wording of its public outputs and add an express
warning that, without distinguishing native THC, THCA and total THC and without
knowledge of sampling and analytical method, some national data may not be directly
comparable. If EUDA refuses such a revision, I request identification of the responsible
person or unit, the scientific basis for that decision and its written reasons.
I request a substantive position on these questions within 30 days.
X. The request for access to documents is separate
 If EUDA holds documents, methodological materials, internal instructions, metadata,
correspondence with National Focal Points or the laboratory network showing how the
comparability of THC data is controlled, I also request access to them under Regulation
(EC) No 1049/2001.
This request for access to documents is separate and does not alter the nature of this
objection.
XI. Further course of action
I regard your response of 25 September 2026 as EUDA’s position on the formal call to act
under Article 265 TFEU. This submission therefore does not suggest that the original
procedural inaction continued after that position was adopted.
The objection now concerns the content and reasoning of EUDA’s position, the
governance of European data, transparency about methodological limitations, and the
manner in which the Agency fulfils its own duties concerning comparability, objectivity
and reliability of data.
In view of the seriousness of the findings contained in EUDA’s response of 25 September
2026, I have submitted the matter for assessment to the European Ombudsman, from the
perspective of possible maladministration in the governance, control, interpretation and
publication of EUDA data and in the performance of the Agency’s duties concerning their
comparability, objectivity and reliability.
I do not ask for the outcome of the scientific assessment to be predetermined.
I ask that EUDA be able to demonstrate the methodological basis of the data it publishes,
explain the limits of their comparability, and transparently state what it knows and does
not know about their comparability.
Yours sincerely,
